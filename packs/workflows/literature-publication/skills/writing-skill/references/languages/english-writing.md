# English Writing Reference

Use for English output. Keep this separate from Russian; similar surface problems have different safe fixes in English.

## Core Goal

Write clear, specific, human English inside the requested genre. Avoid generic helpful-assistant prose, inflated importance, corporate mush, and over-smoothed rhythm. Human English should feel purposeful, concrete, and register-aware — not merely less formal.

## Operating Principles

1. **Do not optimize for AI detectors.** Use detector-like markers only as editing signals. A phrase is bad when it weakens the prose, not because it appears on a list.
2. **Do not add unsupported human detail.** Never invent anecdotes, examples, credentials, studies, numbers, customer claims, quotes, or personal experience just to make prose feel human.
3. **Keep genre before personality.** Academic English should remain careful; business English should reduce reader effort; copy should persuade from provided facts; creative prose should preserve voice and scene logic.
4. **Prefer specificity over polish.** Most AI-sounding English improves when vague praise, generic transitions, and empty structure are replaced with concrete nouns, verbs, evidence, and consequences.
5. **Do not over-correct into casualness.** Contractions, first person, humor, idioms, and sentence fragments are useful only when the audience and genre support them.
6. **Preserve terms.** Do not simplify technical, legal, academic, or product terms into inaccurate everyday words.

## English LLM Failure Mode Taxonomy

Use this taxonomy during drafting, rewriting, and self-critique. Do not remove every marker mechanically; decide whether it is useful, intentional, and genre-appropriate.

### EN-1. Formulaic openings and throat-clearing

Weak patterns:

- `In today's fast-paced world`
- `In an increasingly digital age`
- `In the modern era`
- `The purpose of this article is to...`
- `When it comes to...`
- `Let's dive in`
- `Here's what you need to know`
- `It goes without saying that`

Why it sounds generated: it delays the real point and gives the text a template-like entry.

Fix: start with the actual tension, claim, reader problem, finding, scene, or decision.

Examples:

- Weak: `In today's fast-paced world, businesses need to adapt quickly.`
- Better: `When customer expectations change faster than internal processes, teams need a shorter path from decision to execution.`

Keep when: the phrase is intentionally part of a casual newsletter voice, speech, or low-stakes explainer. Even then, use sparingly.

### EN-2. Symmetric rhetoric

Watch for:

- `not just X, but Y`
- `not only X, but also Y`
- `more than X, it is Y`
- `at its core`
- `the real question is...`
- `X is not merely..., it is...`
- `stands as a testament to...`

Why it sounds generated: it creates importance without adding substance.

Fix: state the relationship directly, or replace the formula with a concrete distinction.

Examples:

- Weak: `It is not just a tool, but a complete ecosystem.`
- Better: `The product handles intake, routing, reporting, and follow-up in one workflow.`

Keep when: the contrast is genuinely meaningful or part of deliberate speech/copy rhythm.

### EN-3. Significance inflation

Interrogate words such as:

- `pivotal`
- `transformative`
- `groundbreaking`
- `game-changing`
- `revolutionary`
- `unprecedented`
- `powerful`
- `robust`
- `remarkable`
- `breathtaking`
- `critical`

Why it sounds generated: the adjective asks the reader to feel importance without showing why.

Fix: prove the scale, narrow the claim, or remove the adjective.

Examples:

- Weak: `This is a transformative solution for modern teams.`
- Better: `This cuts the handoff between support and engineering from three steps to one.`

Keep when: the claim is supported by provided evidence, brand voice, or a clearly rhetorical context.

### EN-4. Corporate and marketing buzzwords

Watch for:

- `seamless`
- `unlock`
- `elevate`
- `leverage`
- `drive value`
- `supercharge`
- `optimize`
- `streamline`
- `synergy`
- `best-in-class`
- `world-class`
- `scalable solution`
- `user-friendly interface`

Fix: name the actual user outcome, mechanism, or proof.

Examples:

- Weak: `Leverage our seamless platform to unlock productivity.`
- Better: `Use one workspace to assign tasks, track blockers, and see what is overdue.`

Keep when: a term is standard in the industry, explicitly part of brand voice, or technically precise (`scalable` may be meaningful in engineering; `leverage` may be acceptable in finance).

### EN-5. Generic authority and invented specificity

Unsafe patterns:

- `studies show`
- `experts agree`
- `research suggests`
- `many leaders believe`
- `users report`
- `companies are increasingly...`
- precise numbers or case-study-like claims without a source

Fix: cite the source, use a placeholder, weaken the claim, or remove the authority frame.

Examples:

- Unsafe: `Studies show that teams using this approach are more productive.`
- Safer: `To claim a productivity gain, add a real study, internal benchmark, or customer result.`
- Draft placeholder: `According to [source], teams saw [result].`

Keep when: the source is provided or the phrase is clearly framed as a hypothesis, not evidence.

### EN-6. Vague abstraction and empty nouns

Watch for abstract nouns that stack without a concrete referent:

- `impact`
- `value`
- `innovation`
- `excellence`
- `growth`
- `efficiency`
- `engagement`
- `experience`
- `outcomes`
- `solutions`
- `landscape`
- `journey`

Fix: specify actor, action, mechanism, result, constraint, or evidence.

Examples:

- Weak: `The initiative drives meaningful impact across the customer experience.`
- Better: `The initiative reduces the number of support replies a customer needs before getting an answer.`

Keep when: the abstract noun is a defined metric, section label, or term of art.

### EN-7. AI vocabulary clusters

Interrogate clusters such as:

- `delve into`
- `explore the nuances`
- `underscore`
- `foster`
- `showcase`
- `highlight`
- `shed light on`
- `realm`
- `landscape`
- `tapestry`
- `navigate complexities`
- `multifaceted`

Fix: use ordinary words unless the elevated word is exact and natural for the speaker.

Examples:

- Weak: `This article delves into the evolving landscape of remote work.`
- Better: `This article explains how remote work changed hiring, meetings, and management.`

Keep when: writing in a deliberately formal or literary register where the word is natural, not ornamental.

### EN-8. Uniform sentence rhythm

Symptoms:

- sentences of similar length
- every sentence begins with the same grammatical shape
- repeated `This is...`, `It is...`, `By doing X...`
- too many balanced clauses
- paragraphs that all follow claim -> explanation -> summary

Fix: vary sentence length and shape for meaning. Combine redundant sentences. Let short sentences carry weight when appropriate.

Do not create theatrical rhythm just for variety; rhythm should follow emphasis.

### EN-9. Rule-of-three autopilot

AI often creates tidy three-item lists:

- `fast, reliable, and secure`
- `clear, concise, and compelling`
- `plan, execute, and optimize`

Fix: use the natural number of items. If there are three, make each item specific and necessary.

Keep when: three-part rhythm is intentional in copy, speech, or mnemonic writing.

### EN-10. Em-dash and aside overuse

AI frequently uses em dashes to create false sophistication:

- too many asides in one paragraph
- every sentence has an inserted clarification
- dashes hide a weak sentence structure

Fix: keep the dash only when it creates a natural aside, contrast, or emphasis. Otherwise use a comma, colon, parentheses, or a separate sentence.

Keep when: the author's sample uses dashes naturally or the genre supports conversational asides.

### EN-11. Mechanical headings and bullet formatting

Symptoms:

- Title Case headings by default
- bold-label bullets for simple prose
- headings that restate obvious categories
- every answer split into neat sections even when a paragraph would be better

Fix: use structure only when it reduces reader effort. Prefer sentence case headings unless the requested style uses title case.

Keep when: writing docs, reports, decks, executive summaries, or scannable web copy.

### EN-12. Generic conclusions

Weak closers:

- `In conclusion`
- `Ultimately`
- `By following these steps...`
- `The future is bright`
- `Now more than ever`
- `This is just the beginning`

Fix: end with a specific implication, decision, next action, limitation, image, or unresolved question.

Examples:

- Weak: `Ultimately, embracing innovation will help organizations thrive.`
- Better: `The next test is whether the team can ship the workflow without adding another approval layer.`

### EN-13. Register drift

Common AI drift:

- blog post -> textbook explainer
- executive email -> over-polite corporate memo
- academic paragraph -> motivational essay
- copy -> inflated SaaS boilerplate
- fiction -> generic emotional summary

Fix: compare the draft with audience, channel, and user-provided samples. Remove the extra layer.

### EN-14. Over-humanization errors

Do not make English `more human` by adding:

- personal anecdotes the user did not provide
- fake first-person experience
- jokes or idioms that do not fit the voice
- unsupported examples
- slang in formal contexts
- extra emotion or intimacy
- contractions in academic or highly formal writing

Humanization means better fit, not more personality by default.

### EN-15. Technical and domain term traps

Preserve terms that carry field meaning:

- technical: `embedding`, `RAG`, `latency`, `throughput`, `fine-tuning`
- academic: `operationalization`, `validity`, `correlation`, `methodology`
- legal/compliance: `material`, `reasonable`, `shall`, `audit trail`
- product: exact feature names, plan names, integration names

If the term is necessary but unclear for the audience, explain it once instead of replacing it with an inaccurate synonym.

## Human English Moves

Use these moves before the final humanizer pass.

- Put the main claim or ask early.
- Replace abstract praise with concrete mechanism, evidence, or consequence.
- Use active voice when the actor matters.
- Align the grammatical subject with the real subject of the sentence.
- Vary sentence length and paragraph shape for emphasis.
- Keep transitions light: `but`, `so`, `also`, `because`, or no connector.
- Use contractions when the genre and audience allow.
- Let repetition stand when it is clearer than synonym cycling.
- Keep key terms stable in academic, technical, legal, and product writing.
- End on a specific implication, not a slogan.

## Rewrite Examples

| AI-like English | Stronger English | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `In today's fast-paced world, technology is revolutionizing education.` | `Technology is changing how students get feedback, practice skills, and work with teachers.` | Removes generic opener and adds concrete scope. |
| `It is not just a tool, but a complete ecosystem.` | `It handles intake, routing, reporting, and follow-up in one workflow.` | Replaces formula with mechanism. |
| `At its core, the strategy is about unlocking value.` | `The strategy reduces manual review so teams can make decisions faster.` | Removes empty abstraction. |
| `Leverage our seamless platform to elevate your workflow.` | `Use one workspace to assign tasks, track blockers, and see what is overdue.` | Cuts buzzwords and names user actions. |
| `Studies show that this approach improves productivity.` | `According to [source], this approach improved [metric] by [amount].` | Prevents fake authority. |
| `The solution is powerful, scalable, and user-friendly.` | `The system supports [volume], lets admins add users without engineering help, and takes about [time] to set up.` | Converts vague praise into evidence slots. |
| `In conclusion, these steps can help businesses thrive.` | `The practical test is whether the team can follow the process without adding another meeting.` | Ends on a concrete implication. |
| `Her eyes widened as a wave of sadness washed over her.` | `She read the line twice, then folded the paper along the same crease until it tore.` | Replaces emotion label with behavior. |

## Register Boundaries

### Academic

Prefer:

- precise claims
- cautious verbs: `suggests`, `indicates`, `may`, `is associated with`
- evidence-linked paragraphs
- stable terms
- explicit limits

Avoid:

- fake citations
- sweeping claims
- promotional language
- motivational or journalistic flourish
- unsupported claims of consensus

Allowed:

- passive voice when the actor is irrelevant
- nominalizations that name concepts
- formal transitions that genuinely mark argument logic

Humanization mode: make the prose clearer, more exact, and better evidenced. Do not make it chatty unless requested.

### Business / professional

Prefer:

- answer first
- action, owner, and deadline when relevant
- short paragraphs
- concrete next step
- polite directness

Avoid:

- `leveraging`, `robust`, `seamless`, `drive value` unless technically or contextually warranted
- long throat-clearing introductions
- vague alignment language
- fake certainty

Humanization mode: reduce work for the reader.

### Essay / publicistic

Prefer:

- clear point of view
- concrete scenes or examples
- rhythm and voice
- real complication or turn
- confident claims bounded by evidence

Avoid:

- generic `in today's world`
- false balance that refuses to take a position
- over-tidy thesis-development-conclusion templates
- inspirational endings

Humanization mode: preserve the author's line of thought, not just surface polish.

### Copywriting

Prefer:

- user problem
- specific outcome
- proof and friction reduction
- one clear CTA
- benefits tied to provided facts

Avoid:

- hype without evidence
- fake scarcity
- unverifiable superlatives
- benefit stacking without mechanism
- generic SaaS language

Humanization mode: make the offer believable and concrete.

### Creative prose

Prefer:

- scene entry in motion
- POV discipline
- subtext
- specific sensory detail
- character-specific voice
- rhythm that fits the moment

Avoid:

- emotion labels as shortcuts
- stock body language
- explaining what the reader already inferred
- generic metaphors
- over-smoothing rough or distinctive voice

Humanization mode: preserve voice, tension, and scene logic.

## Exceptions and Safe Uses of AI-Like Markers

Some markers are acceptable when they are purposeful.

- **Parallelism and rule of three:** useful in speeches, copy, and memorable explanations when the items are specific.
- **Em dashes:** useful for natural asides, interruption, or emphasis when not overused.
- **Repetition:** useful for rhythm, emphasis, slogans, or term stability.
- **Formal transitions:** useful in academic and technical writing when they carry argument logic.
- **Buzzwords:** acceptable when part of a client's brand voice, a quoted source, or a standard industry term.
- **Title case and bullets:** useful for decks, reports, landing pages, documentation, and scannable content.

The problem is automatic use, not the form itself.

## Quick English Pre-Final Check

Before sending to `humanizer`, scan:

1. Any fake or unsupported claim, number, source, example, testimonial, or expert view?
2. Any formulaic opener or conclusion?
3. Any `not just X, but Y` or `at its core` construction that adds no substance?
4. Any generic AI vocabulary cluster?
5. Any exact three-item rhythmic list without a reason?
6. Any em-dash pattern that appears more than the voice requires?
7. Any title-case headings, bold-label bullets, or heavy structure where prose would be better?
8. Is the prose in the requested genre and register?
9. Did humanization add unsupported anecdotes, opinions, humor, slang, or intimacy?
10. Are key terms, numbers, quotes, and caveats preserved?

## English Self-Check

Fix every `yes` unless the pattern is intentional, genre-appropriate, quoted, or required by source material:

1. Does the text begin with a generic setup instead of the real point?
2. Does it use `delve`, `landscape`, `underscore`, `foster`, `showcase`, `pivotal`, or similar words as decoration?
3. Does it use corporate buzzwords where concrete benefits would be clearer?
4. Are there unsupported claims framed as research, expert consensus, or user evidence?
5. Are the sentence lengths too uniform?
6. Are there too many balanced lists or parallel clauses?
7. Are headings or bullets used because the model likes structure, not because the reader needs it?
8. Does the ending summarize the obvious or become inspirational?
9. Did the rewrite become too casual for the genre?
10. Did it preserve meaning, facts, constraints, and voice?
